Introduction

Call me Scholar.

Some years ago—never mind how many—when I had a year left in university, I took an anthropology module called CATASTROPHE. I don't remember most of the course's contents, as it has been years.

Given the climate and growing concerns around AI, fear around its proliferation, and achieving general intelligence (matching or surpassing human capabilities), it felt like a great time to revisit some of those readings on societal fears around catastrophe and doomsday scenarios and write something on AI doom and gloom.

Understanding AI Anxiety

So, let's start with understanding what I mean by AI anxiety. AI anxiety could be defined as a generalized fear around AI, its proliferation, and what this means for us as a species. I don't want to be hasty in prescribing what the scope of this should be or look like, but I would define it as a range from "I'm fearful of my job being replaced" to cataclysmic meltdown levels of fear/paranoia and anxiety.

This AI anxiety has been around for much longer, even though widespread everyday use of AI only really took off towards the end of 2022 with the release of ChatGPT. In short, AI anxiety has a greater range than Matthew McConaughey's.

This anxiety has been fueled by speculative works like Nick Bostrom's 'Superintelligence' and discussions around the uncontrollable nature of advanced AI technologies." Bostrom explores scenarios where AI could surpass human intelligence and control, posing existential risks if not properly managed. His analysis underscores the need for rigorous oversight and developing aligned, beneficial AI systems to mitigate these threats. If you’re unfamiliar with Nick Bostrom, he’s a philosopher who has become more popular due to writing on AI and its existential risks.

Eliezer Yudkowsky, similar to Bostrom has also written work on AI. Yudkowsky is a researcher focused on AI safety and risks, and is well-known for his writings on the potential global catastrophic risks posed by AI. Yudkowsky's piece, "Artificial intelligence as a positive and negative factor in global risk," evaluates AI potential in terms of global catastrophic risk. Yudkowsky discusses how AI, if not carefully controlled, could act unpredictably, leading to scenarios where its goals misalign with human values, thus creating substantial risks. These concerns reflect a broader apprehension about the uncontrollable nature of advanced AI technologies and their potential to cause unintended harm on a massive scale.

As AI seemingly integrates more deeply into our digital infrastructure and collective imagination, concerns over cybersecurity and the potential for AI-driven attacks have grown, contributing to a broader sense of AI anxiety. Concerns over the insecurity of cybersecurity and threats to cyberspace (including AI) are inscribed within cyber discourse: scholars, politicians, and security officials have voiced growing unease over lacking cybersecurity and AI regulations. Discussion on threat proliferation plays a vital role in shaping the collective imagination of these threats. If we analyze this discourse further, there is an apparent emergent fear of some level of cataclysmic meltdown or catastrophe.

Cyber and AI anxiety and doom seem to be lurking on the horizon within this perspective. It's helpful to illustrate examples of what could be fuelling the fear around AI attacks, like some actual case studies of AI-driven cyber attacks.

Two Really Short Examples of What Has Contributed to ‘Our’ Fears (because I'm lazy)

In 2017, the world witnessed one of the most destructive cyberattacks in history: the NotPetya ransomware attack. Unlike traditional ransomware, which aims to extort money from victims by encrypting their data and demanding a ransom for decryption, NotPetya was designed to cause maximum disruption and destruction. This attack leveraged advanced AI techniques to enhance its propagation and impact.

NotPetya initially spread through a Ukrainian accounting software called MeDoc. Once inside a system, it exploited the EternalBlue vulnerability, the same exploit used by the WannaCry ransomware earlier in 2017. (If you’re curious, the EternalBlue vulnerability was a security flaw in Windows operating systems, in its Server Message Block (SMB) protocol, which allowed malicious actors to execute code and gain unauthorized access to networked systems, which facilitated the rapid spread of malware and ransomware across unpatched and vulnerable computers). However, what set NotPetya apart was its use of AI-driven mechanisms to spread across networks and evade traditional security measures rapidly. The malware used a combination of credential-stealing techniques and lateral movement strategies, enabling it to infect entire networks within hours.

The AI component of NotPetya allowed it to adapt its attack methods dynamically. By analyzing the network environment in real-time, NotPetya could choose the most effective propagation techniques, making it highly resilient and difficult to contain. This adaptability and resilience significantly damaged global corporations, including Maersk, Merck, and FedEx, causing billions of dollars in losses.

More recently, we saw cybercriminals exploiting generative AI to launch even more sophisticated attacks. The 2024 CrowdStrike Global Threat Report highlights how adversaries leverage stolen identity credentials to exploit gaps in cloud environments and maximize their cyberattack's stealth, speed, and impact. This includes a dramatic increase in the velocity of attacks, with some breaches occurring in under three minutes once initial access is obtained.

Generative AI is being used to create more convincing and personalized phishing attacks, making it easier for even novice cybercriminals to execute complex and believable scams. This technology has lowered the barrier to entry for cybercriminals, allowing them to automate and enhance their attack methods significantly. The report also underscores the growing threat of AI-driven disinformation campaigns, particularly in the context of global elections, where AI-generated images and videos are used to mislead and manipulate public opinion.

Modern AI Threats and Concerns

AI presents unprecedented opportunities and significant threats in today's rapidly evolving digital landscape. Here are some up-to-date AI threats and their implications:

  1. Deepfakes: AI-generated deepfake videos and audio have the potential to create compelling fake content, posing threats to privacy, security, and political stability. Malicious actors can use deepfakes to impersonate individuals, spread disinformation, and manipulate public opinion. For example, deepfake technology was used to create a video of a CEO giving fraudulent instructions to employees, leading to significant financial losses.
  2. Autonomous Weapon Systems: AI-driven autonomous weapons capable of making decisions without human intervention and raising ethical and security concerns. These systems can be hacked or malfunction, leading to unintended casualties and escalation of conflicts.
  3. AI-Powered Phishing Attacks: Cybercriminals increasingly use AI to craft highly personalized phishing emails. By analyzing social media profiles and other online data, AI can generate convincing messages more likely to deceive recipients into divulging sensitive information.
  4. Adversarial AI: Adversarial attacks involve manipulating AI systems by introducing subtle, malicious inputs that cause the AI to make incorrect decisions. These attacks can compromise the integrity of AI models in critical applications, such as autonomous driving, healthcare, and finance.

Securitization Theory and AI Doom

I also want to touch on securitization theory to better explain how discursive practices (which means people talking/writing about it) have contributed to this fear on a population basis (for those of us who are chronically online) beyond the risks it actually poses. Securitization theory, in essence, is an academic theory that discusses how issues become framed as security threats through discourse, emphasizing the role of language and communication in constructing perceived threats - or how important people yapping make an issue more impactful through writing or talking about it. Like other technological advancements, AI has been hailed as a revolutionary tool and equally feared as a harbinger of doom. Concerns over AI's potential to cause catastrophic events have been echoed by prominent figures in technology and security, contributing to a growing discourse on AI doom and gloom.

Our relationship with this fear can be well contextualized with the academic work on nuclear fallout and its associated fears. Our conceptualizations of time are also inherently linked to cyber and AI anxiety. Also, ruptures in time tied to the notion of catastrophe can be used to understand better how specific events have impacted the public imagination and the fear of the AI apocalypse.

AI in Cybersecurity

The emergence of AI in cybersecurity has added a new layer to existing anxieties. AI systems are now being used to detect and respond to cyber threats more quickly and efficiently than human operators ever could. However, malicious actors can also use the same AI technologies to launch more sophisticated and harder-to-detect attacks. This dual-use nature of AI creates a paradox where it is both a safeguard and a potential threat. For instance, AI can automate the discovery of zero-day vulnerabilities, which can then be exploited to cause widespread damage before patches are developed. The inherent unpredictability and potential for rapid escalation in AI-driven cyber conflicts contribute to the growing fear and anxiety surrounding these technologies.

AI tools can be weaponized to create autonomous systems capable of conducting cyberattacks without human intervention. For example, AI could create self-propagating malware that learns from its environment and adapts its tactics to evade detection and maximize damage. This potential for autonomous, adaptive cyber threats heightens the stakes in cybersecurity, making it a more complex and potentially more dangerous field.

Securitization of Cyber and AI Threats

Securitization theory, whereby discursive acts can constitute and perpetuate a threat, illustrates how cyber and AI anxiety have become issues. This paper recognizes that securitization is an essentially contested concept. However, in this paper, securitization can be understood as public issues proposed as existential threats. Furthermore, securitization can be analyzed directly through discourse. Securitization can be analyzed regarding how malware and cyber threats are referred to in media discourse, specifically by those with expert knowledge. Academic George R. Lucas writes, "Ours is the age of cyber anxiety". Lucas alone does not posit this cyber anxiety, which securitizes the subject in the works and speeches of many others.

Frank L. Smith III writes about what he calls "The Malware Pandemic" and compares malware to biological viruses or worms, rendering malware like Stuxnet or Flame the equivalent of grave human contagion. IT expert Brian Laing refers to cybersecurity as a state of meltdown and thereby circumscribes it with this securitizing move.*(there are other more current examples) In a Forbes article, cybercrime and data breaches are perceived to be more devastating to businesses than floods, fires, and transit strikes combined. Even the securitization of Stuxnet alone has impacted growing cyber concerns. Stuxnet has gained infamy in security specialist circles and was called "the most menacing software" and "the most sophisticated cyber weapon ever deployed". The language describing this malware reflects its presentation as a threat securitized through discourse. Furthermore, comparisons to nuclear weapons have only further contributed to the securitization of cybersecurity threats.

Similarly, the threat of AI has been securitized, primarily via social media/online news outlets that need to get more clicks via sensationalizing and fear-mongering. We've seen AI and fear-mongering around it from industry giants like Elon Musk (and whether or not you like him as a person or agree with him, his opinions are weighty and DO affect public opinions). This securitization is evident in articles like "Elon Musk predicts AI will overtake humans to the point that 'biological intelligence will be 1%'" and others. We're also seeing a slew of other articles now, in response, that are addressing the contrary, saying it does not pose much of a threat.

AI anxiety also stems from the fear of catastrophic scenarios where AI systems could go rogue or be used maliciously. The idea of a "technological singularity," where AI surpasses human intelligence and begins to improve itself autonomously, is a prominent theme in discussions about AI catastrophes and popular media. This scenario, popularized by Bostrom and others, posits that once AI reaches a certain level of sophistication, it could rapidly evolve beyond our control, leading to outcomes that are impossible to predict and potentially disastrous for humanity.

Cybersecurity and Nuclear Comparisons + How Our Conception of Time is Linked to This Feeling of Doom

Security specialist expert Brian Mazanec theorizes that nations are at the same precipice of cyber conflict as they once were with nuclear. Mazanec draws comparisons with nuclear weapons because cyber weapons, like nuclear, can devastate populations at similarly astounding rates. Mazanec's comparison to nuclear weapons is an example of the securitization of cyber weapons in the academic sphere. Securitization theory elucidates how critical discourse shapes the public imagination and contributes to the growing cyber anxiety. The securitization of cyberattacks is linked to the belief in cyber-apocalypse. The belief in the dangers of cyberattacks can be understood better through literature on fears of nuclear fallout. There are similarities in both nuclear and cyber anxieties, given the uncertainty inherent to both these situations.

Anthropologist Joseph Masco (known for his work on nuclear and cyber security and their impacts) notes that 'fallout' does not fall within a contained time period but comes after an event. Specifically, fallout remains undetected until it occurs as a disaster that is embedded within the infrastructure of a system, program, or process. This concept of fallout is relevant not only to nuclear warfare but also to cyber warfare. Both Stuxnet and Flame demonstrate the unacknowledged until occurring crisis, given Iran's lack of preparedness for the devastation these programs cause. Arguably, had Iran been able to anticipate the cyberattacks, there would have been no such fallout. With increasing reliance on cyberspace-enabled infrastructures and devices, the potential for fallout becomes more devastating.

Masco also notes a critical aspect of understanding fallout is its relationship with time. Though time is understood retrospectively, it poses an immediate threat at present while posing long-term threats simultaneously. The situatedness of the injury of fallout, or the long-term effects, "installs injury and crises incrementally into the future, colonizing an ever deeper time horizon". Though Masco refers to the effects of nuclear fallout and how the nuclear industries and processes have colonized the future while posing immediate threats, the perception of cyber danger and cyber fallout is no different. Cyberattacks in their gravest forms pose a severe threat; for example, they can cause deaths through the chemical treatment of drinking water plants and long-term effects like economic, infrastructure, and political harm through disabling power grids. Gusterson's writing on nuclear weapons also further elucidates certain aspects of cyber anxiety. Anthropological literature focusing on risk reveals that shared fears reveal much about the identities and commonalities of those who fear the threat. The shared panic amongst academics, experts, and government officials has been shaped by the same discourse securitizing cybersecurity. Through this discourse and understanding of fallout, cyber anxiety has been shared and perpetuated. Furthermore, cyberattacks have changed how people configure and imagine their collective futures. Cyberattacks have forced reconceptualizations of time and space, adding to unease about the future.

The nature of cyber anxiety and its inherent temporal quality make clear that homogenous understandings of time are inapplicable for its socially constructed nature. If we examine anthropological writing on continuity and time or another Western scholar'’ assumption of time as continued and uninterrupted - we can kind of see how these conceptions of time have been impacted by cyber-doom. Time was traditionally theorized as homogenous, empty time. However, this 'even' time cannot account for ruptures in time or understandings that have divisive epochs based on their quality rather than their quantity. For example, in Christianity, its conception of time allows heterogeneous or uneven time to exist, wherein transformation results from these ruptures.

The age of cyber weapons or, more specifically, the age after certain malware is an example of such a rupturing. The cyberattacks by both Stuxnet and Flame have altered what was previously conceptualized as an even, homogeneous time. Cyberattacks have revealed the linear continuity of time is unhelpful in characterizing the cybersecurity paradigm. Cyberattacks ruptured historical consciousness, producing political transformation in the same way 9/11 did. 9/11 has often been analyzed as an event which has divided time into the post and pre-9/11 world order and political practices. It was an apocalyptic event in its formation of a temporal break which altered imaginings of futures. Stuxnet caused a similar rupture in the cybersecurity consciousness; there is a time now and a time after the 'first genuine' cyber weapon. This understanding of time is inherently tied to an understanding of the cyber-apocalypse or the catastrophe of cybersecurity. And to be sure, we will see a similar event with AI weaponry.

We can see the same type of break-in time with the innovation and increased adoption of advanced AI systems. The period before and after the rise of AI technologies like ChatGPT marks a significant transformation. The rapid advancements in AI have altered the landscape of what is possible, causing a shift in how we perceive future risks and the potential for catastrophic events. This transformation in our own temporal perception is crucial for understanding AI anxiety, as it underscores the unpredictability and potential magnitude of AI-related threats.

Imagination and Prophetic Visions

Some anxiety over our cyber world and AI originates from a lack of imagination over what the future will look like, as uncertainties and unknowns populate it; we really can't account for much and black swan events are pretty good indications of this. Uncertainty over the future has created and added to the prophetic visions of cyber-doom. Insecurities over unclear and ambiguous futures also inform this paranoia in that these insecurities are relegated to a vague future, ultimately adding to the projection of the future as reduced to catastrophic events. This emphasis on ruinous events in a distant or near but characteristically unclear future is quintessential to understanding this feeling of doom and gloom. Commonly, these catastrophic events in cybersecurity are hyper-securitized in discursive practices.

For example, Elon Musk has referred to general technology and AI as the beginnings of a techno-apocalypse. Other articles in media that are readily accessible to the public are titled things like "Cyber Apocalypse Now" and detail worst-case scenarios for a cyber-apocalypse while also detailing the present injury of cyber fallout. Whether or not these cases of cyber-doom involving the collapse of infrastructure are possible or probable does not erase their presence and presumed immanence. Moreover, cybersecurity threats are constructed through discourse with a characteristic apocalyptic aesthetic. These threats are all part of the construction of the catastrophic cyber event, with terms like "cybergeddon," "cyber armageddon," and even "cyber-apocalypse" as part of the discursive practice around cybersecurity. This continued sense of crisis in cybersecurity discourse makes it clear why the issue has been prophesized as ending with a cataclysmic meltdown.

Conclusion

Understanding the link between apocalyptic catastrophe and cyber/AI anxiety requires a nuanced, multi-faceted approach. The evolution of malware, illustrated by the case study of the NotPetya attack, demonstrated how AI-driven cyber assaults can cause widespread disruption and damage. The recent exploitation of generative AI, as reported in the 2024 CrowdStrike Global Threat Report, underscores how cybercriminals leverage AI to enhance their attacks' stealth, speed, and impact. Modern AI threats, such as deepfakes, autonomous weapon systems, AI-powered phishing attacks, and adversarial AI, pose significant risks to privacy, security, and political stability. However, we must evaluate these risks to understand how much discursive practices can create a sense of globular fear. Our discussion on securitization theory explained how discursive practices contribute to the collective fear of AI-induced catastrophes, drawing parallels to the concerns associated with nuclear fallout. Temporal ruptures caused by advancements in AI have altered our perception of time and security, marking a significant transformation in how we view future risks. The framing of cybersecurity within the context of modernity has further fueled anxiety about the future. The uncertainty and lack of imagination about AI's potential have also led to prophetic visions of cyber doom, emphasizing catastrophic events in our collective imagination.

If we are to be fearful about anything, it's the amount of data we are giving away freely and, depending on the country you live in, sometimes by force. Let's be less fearful of a "Westworld" experience and more vigilant about the data we share and the safeguards we implement to protect against these evolving threats. Furthermore, we should be more wary of how our fellow man will weaponize AI rather than fearing it suddenly gaining sentience and becoming evil. Human misuse of AI technologies presents a far more immediate and tangible threat than the speculative idea of AI turning against us.
