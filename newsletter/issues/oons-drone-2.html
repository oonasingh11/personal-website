I. The Asset Model

The first thing I did was purchase an asset package for my drone model. In Unity, an asset package resembles a library or a gem in Rails. Just like gems provide pre-built functionalities or tools in Ruby on Rails, Unity asset packages provide ready-made models, textures, scripts, and other assets you can use in your game development without starting from scratch.

I used the Unity Hub's store to find and purchase this asset model, which was a straightforward process. I selected a model based on its specs (which I didn’t fully understand before this exercise). For reference, here are the specs of the model I purchased (feel free to skip the next section through to the next * if you’re not interested in the details):

“Polygons: 2,592

  • Vertices: 2,581
  • Textures: Color, Metallic, Roughness, Normal, AO
  • Resolution: All textures are 2k
  • UV Mapping: Yes
  • PBR (Physically Based Rendering): Yes
  • Geometry Type: Polygon mesh
  • Unwrapped UVs: Mixed
  • Customization Options: Wheels and shafts are separate objects, so they could be turned off/removed for "in-flight" mode.
  • Model Association: Not tied to any existing UCAV design, so no worries about intellectual property.”

II. Model Specs

What does this mean?

If you remember from your geometry class, polygons - typically triangles in game development, are the foundation of 3D models. A polygon count of 2592 is low compared to other models, which is favorable for performance, especially as I'm running this simulation on my MacBook, which has capped processing power.

Vertices are probably also familiar to you. They are points in 3D space that define the corners or junctions of polygons; similarly, fewer vertices mean better performance.

The model is well-optimized for a game environment because it has a low polygon and vertex count. This makes it possible for real-time rendering in Unity.

The specs also include 5 textures, which determine the color, the metallic quality of its surfaces, how well it reflects light, and the ability to add grooves or bumps without increasing the polygon count (which enhances realism without increasing the required processing power for running the sim), and shadows to add to the depth.

These textures provide a high level of detail and realism to the model. A 2k resolution is usually balanced, offering good visual quality without too taxing on performance. The different texture maps (Metallic, Roughness, Normal, AO) help achieve a realistic look by accurately simulating various surface properties.

This model also has flexibility because the wheels and shafts can be removed or adjusted as they are ornamental.

UV Mapping, which this model has, is how textures are used on its 3D surface. Good UV mapping ensures that the model's textures (like colors and surface details) look natural.

PBR, or Physically Based Rendering materials, is how the light interacts with surfaces, including its metallicity, roughness, and surface normals. PBR is necessary because it makes the drone look realistic in different lighting conditions. The polygon mesh geometry this model uses is the standard for 3D models in games (it utilizes interconnected polygons used for models).

III. The Drone in Action and Some Bad Physics

So, after purchasing the model, I imported it into my Unity project, so my screen looked like this:

This is pretty cool. We have our drone on our screen. The next thing to do, to enable all the physics interactions of the model, was to set up the Rigidbody component. The Rigidbody component in Unity allows our drone to interact with the physics engine, letting it respond to forces like gravity, drag, and collisions realistically.

That is what the variables of the Rigidbody component look like:

If you remember from your physics class(if you took one) - we have mass (which I set to 8000kg as a more realistic representation of this type of UCAV drone's weight). However, I faced a problem when initially setting this up: I didn't consider how much proportionate force I would need to move or stop it; more on that later.

Drag is a force opposing an object's motion; Unity here simulates aerodynamic resistance. A higher drag value means the drone should decelerate quickly when you stop applying thrust, giving it a sense of realism — like it's moving through air. A lower drag value will make the drone glide further with less resistance.

Angular Drag is a property that affects how quickly an object stops rotating. It represents the rotational equivalent of linear drag, simulating the air resistance or friction that opposes rotational motion. This property in Unity affects how much rotational resistance the drone experiences. Adjusting this value can help balance how quickly the drone stops turning after applying a rotation force, leading to more realistic and controllable flight behavior.
