AI CapEx

in short - are we spending too much money on AI?

Hello all!

Welcome and welcome back.

I was present for an exciting conversation many months ago during my sojourn in New York—about AI, but not in the purely technological sense. Instead, this conversation was about the capital expenditure (CapEx) in AI and what that meant for finance.

If you want to read something (relatively quickly) that'll give you some extra stuff to think about on the topic of AI—this is that newsletter.

Let us begin.

Introduction: The AI CapEx Debate

The rapid escalation of capital expenditure (CapEx) in artificial intelligence (AI) has ignited a (not-so) global debate: Are we witnessing a transformative technological revolution, or are we on the brink of an economic bubble reminiscent of past speculative frenzies? This newsletter will examine the multifaceted dimensions of AI, exploring its strategic underpinnings, inherent risks, historical parallels, and the fine line between innovation and speculation.

Simply put, AI CapEx involves massive investments in computing hardware, data centers, and advanced algorithms needed to train and deploy AI models. We’ve seen tech giants like Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and Apple at the forefront of this investment surge, each striving to secure a dominant position in the new AI landscape. This zealous fervor has been likened to an "AI arms race," where the stakes seem high (and expensive), with a relentless and escalatory pace. For example, Nvidia reported a 94% year-over-year increase in revenue for Q3 2024, reaching $35.1 billion, driven by a heightened demand for AI-related hardware.

A central question emerges: Is this aggressive investment justified by the anticipated benefits of AI CapEx, or are we inflating a bubble poised to pop? Goldman Sachs projects that AI investments could reach $1 trillion in the coming years, yet there are growing concerns about whether the returns will align with these hefty expenditures. This dichotomy between optimism and skepticism forms the crux of this current discourse on AI CapEx.

The AI Arms Race and Game Theory

To understand the competitive dynamics of AI investments, it's useful to draw on concepts from game theory—particularly the idea of an arms race. Traditionally, an arms race refers to escalating competition between nations to amass superior military capabilities. In the context of AI, this term describes big corporations' rapid accumulation of AI resources and capabilities.

A defining feature of any arms race is its compressed escalation timeline, where entities are compelled to match or surpass their competitors’ advancements imminently. In the AI domain, this translates to accelerated investments in hardware, software, and human capital to avoid falling behind.

In game theory, a Nash equilibrium occurs when players, aware of each other's strategies, have no incentive to deviate from their chosen course. In the AI arms race, companies find themselves in a Nash equilibrium, where reducing investment could result in a loss of competitive edge. In this instance, if Amazon were to scale back its AI spending while Google continues to invest heavily, Amazon could risk ceding market share. This scenario creates a self-reinforcing loop of escalating investments, further galvanizing companies to keep pace.

So, there’s an impetus to keep going on the development side for these companies—but what about the risks? Isn’t this continued innovation good? Indeed, increased spending and energy in AI ensures rapid innovation. What are the downsides? I'm glad you asked!

(Tim Hamilton, 2024).

Key Risks in AI CapEx

Computing Overhang

One significant risk in the AI arms race is the computing overhang—the overinvestment in computing hardware that may exceed actual needs. Companies invest heavily in GPUs, TPUs, and expansive data centers to support AI initiatives (read my chips article if you want a refresher). However, if AI models do not advance as rapidly as anticipated, much of this infrastructure could remain underutilized, leading to extreme financial inefficiencies. Nvidia’s dominance in the AI chip market has created “pricing inefficiencies” as demand for GPUs outstrips supply, leading to delays in AI projects globally and escalating competition among companies to secure hardware. Nvidia’s 2025 production of its highly anticipated Blackwell chips is reportedly sold out, illustrating the growing challenge of meeting escalating demand.

AI, for example, has garnered substantial attention but may not fully meet its hype. People on TikTok promote ChatGPT as the solution for scheduling and productivity woes. In the venture space, companies showcase AI models that provide exciting demos but are very much the "Emperor’s New Clothes" in practice.

If any of these tools prove to be more "flash and glitter" than substance, companies will face the financial consequences of overinvestment. Economist Daron Acemoglu, in his paper "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI," cautions that the economic impact of AI could be slower and more limited than many anticipate, highlighting the risk of speculative frenzy leading to wasted resources. This is a profound point, as outsiders to these companies, we can only observe these cool and exciting new products—while the years and years of development and testing it took to get here are outside our ken.

Bottlenecks in Chips and Power

Another critical risk in AI CapEx is bottlenecks in chips and power. The inflated valuation of Nvidia, which soared to over $1 trillion in market capitalization in 2023, underscores the significance of AI-related hardware. However, this rapid demand for GPUs has led to supply shortages, increasing prices and delaying projects globally. The energy demands of data centers are another bottleneck. For example, AI training for large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4 reportedly requires megawatts of power, comparable to running a small city. Goldman Sachs noted that these shortages could constrain AI growth by up to 20% annually unless alternative solutions, such as energy-efficient chips, are developed.

Historical Parallels

Speculative Frenzy and Historical Harbingers

The enthusiasm surrounding AI has drawn comparisons to past speculative bubbles. In his seminal work A Short History of Financial Euphoria, John Kenneth Galbraith notes that "all crises have involved debt that, in one fashion or another, has become dangerously out of scale in relation to the underlying means of payment." This observation underscores the tendency of speculative booms to detach from economic fundamentals, leading to eventual downturns. Let’s look at two different episodes of mania (very briefly).

Railway Mania

In the 1840s, Britain experienced the Railway Mania, a speculative frenzy over railway construction. Enticed by the promise of high returns, investors financed numerous railway projects, many of which were redundant or poorly planned. Despite financial ruin, surviving railway networks laid the foundation for future industrial and economic growth.

(John Leech, for Punch Magazine - 1849).

The Dot-Com Bubble

The late 1990s witnessed the dot-com bubble, characterized by speculative investments in internet-based companies. Investors poured capital into startups with unproven business models, driven by fear of missing out on the next big thing. When the bubble burst, the resulting infrastructure laid the groundwork for the modern internet economy.

(Farley Katz, for the New Yorker).

Optimism and Opportunities

Despite all my hesitation, I believe AI is a very exciting technology with incredible potential. And perhaps, the timeline of cloud computing offers an instructive parallel. Companies like AWS and Microsoft Azure initially faced skepticism but have since become integral to the digital economy. Microsoft reported a 20% year-over-year growth in its Intelligent Cloud segment, reaching $31.8 billion in Q3 2024, reflecting the surging demand for AI-powered solutions. Much like how cloud computing became a cornerstone of modern business, AI data centers and specialized chips may also become indispensable, even if adoption takes longer than expected.

As AI CapEx grows, stakeholders must navigate a landscape fraught with risks and opportunities. Overinvestment in speculative technologies could lead to financial losses, but the potential for long-term transformation remains immense. The challenge lies in balancing short-term expectations with long-term vision.

Conclusion

The question remains: Is AI a bubble or actually markedly different? Some historical parallels suggest that speculative excesses are likely, but this expenditure could set the stage for future invention. A measured approach focusing on scalable infrastructure is essential. AI’s potential to drive economic and societal progress is enormous—but realizing this potential requires more prudent planning and execution.

AI embodies the promise of transformative innovation mingled with the classic risks of speculative excess. However, we may still be able to navigate this weird frontier and utilize AI’s full potential if we are a little cautious. Whether AI leads to a bubble or a breakthrough, one thing is sure: it will impact the future in profound and, certainly, unpredictable ways.

What about you, reader? What do you think—bubble or breakthrough?

(None of this is financial advice.)
